# Who am I

A brainstorm for good, started 2026-10-23 in the middle of Good5: every name and number a person can meet about their own computer and connection, where each comes from, how hard it is to get, and whether it belongs in the panel, the page, both, or neither. A few are easy enough to bring along on the current trail beside the milestone they fit, most wait for the productization that follows Good10, and some never come.

## The story

A person looks at their connection somewhere else first. A what's-my-IP site names their provider; `ifconfig` or `ipconfig /all` prints a screen of interfaces and addresses; the network pages deep in their system's settings show a MAC address, a router, a lease. Most of it means nothing to them. Then they come to good, and every one of those names and numbers is there, and more, each with what it is, so their learning carries on rather than ending at a number they can't read.

The test: a person who finds a name or number about their connection anywhere else, on a website, from a common command, or deep in a settings page, finds it in good too. The ledger has to be a superset, so good is the one stop, with no bigger or better picture anywhere else. Some of what follows has nothing to do with whether a connection is good for peer-to-peer, and belongs anyway, because a person who can't find it in good goes looking somewhere else.

## The computer

- **Operating system and version.** On a Mac `sw_vers` answers the product, version, and build. No call answers a release's name, so a small table in code holds the last three or so macOS names by major version, and Windows 10 and 11 by build. Windows keeps its version in the registry's CurrentVersion key, DisplayVersion, CurrentBuild, and UBR, the patch number, where ProductName still says Windows 10 on Windows 11, so the build decides, 22000 and up being 11. Linux has `/etc/os-release`, except inside the Flatpak, where that file is the runtime's and the host's is at `/run/host/os-release`. The panel, easy, now.
- **Machine model.** On a Mac `ioreg` answers the words About This Mac shows, and `sysctl hw.model` the model identifier. Windows keeps the maker and model under the registry's BIOS key, SystemManufacturer and SystemProductName, read the same way as the version. Linux has `/sys/class/dmi/id/sys_vendor` and `product_name`. The panel, easy, now.
- **Time since boot, and since the last wake.** `kern.boottime` and `kern.waketime` from `sysctl` on a Mac, `/proc/uptime` on Linux, and GetTickCount64 on Windows, which takes a small Rust command. Windows' Fast Startup makes Shut down hibernate the kernel, so on Windows time since boot resets only on Restart. The panel, easy, now.
- **ftorrent's own start, and both process ids.** The page loads as the process starts, so its own clock's origin is within moments of it, and the engine can say when it started in its ready line. `process_id` and `engine_status` answer the ids, as About shows them. The panel, easy, now.
- **Computer name.** The name in the system's sharing settings, the `.local` name, and the name the computer gives the router's DHCP server: `scutil --get ComputerName` and `LocalHostName` on a Mac, `hostname` on Linux, and GetComputerNameEx on Windows. The panel, easy.
- **Interfaces and addresses.** Good3 lists every interface and every address with what each is. What `ipconfig /all` prints that the panel doesn't yet: the DNS suffix search list, the node type, NetBIOS over TCP/IP, and each adapter's DHCPv6 IAID and client DUID, the two numbers people search for most. The panel, with the DHCP lease below.
- **MAC addresses.** Each interface's hardware address: getifaddrs lists one per interface, which `net_interfaces` passes over today, and Windows has it as the adapter's PhysicalAddress. macOS's private Wi-Fi address and Windows' random hardware addresses make one up for each network, and a made-up address sets the locally administered bit, the second-lowest bit of the first byte, so the panel can say whether an address is the hardware's own or private to this network, which is the one the router sees. The panel, easy, now.
- **The maker behind a MAC address.** The first three bytes, the OUI, are assigned by the IEEE, whose registry runs to tens of thousands of entries. The makers of home routers and network chips are a short list, around twenty for routers, like Sagemcom, Arris, Technicolor, Nokia, Huawei, ZTE, Sercomm, Askey, AVM, Zyxel, Netgear, TP-Link, ASUS, Linksys, Ubiquiti, and eero, and a handful for chips, like Intel, Realtek, Broadcom, Qualcomm, MediaTek, and Apple. Each holds many prefixes, though, so a script filters the IEEE's published file to those makers at build time, a generated table no one keeps by hand. The answer is often the maker of the box rather than the brand on it, and a private address has no maker, which the panel says. The page can't see a MAC address at all. The panel, productization.
- **Link speed and kind.** Ethernet's negotiated speed, 1 gigabit or 2.5 or 10: the media line of `ifconfig` on a Mac, `/sys/class/net/<interface>/speed` on Linux, and the adapter's transmit and receive link speeds from GetAdaptersAddresses on Windows. Wi-Fi's band, 2.4, 5, or 6 GHz, its channel and width, its standard, like 802.11ax, its signal and noise, and its transmit rate: `system_profiler SPAirPortDataType` gives all of them on a Mac without asking permission; Windows has its Native Wifi API, where whether recent Windows 11 asks for location for these, as it does for the network's name, is to check; Linux has `iw`. The panel, medium, the basics maybe now.
- **The Wi-Fi network's name and its access point's address.** macOS answers both as `<redacted>` unless the app has Location permission, and recent Windows 11 does the same for `netsh wlan`. A BitTorrent client shouldn't ask for Location, so never, and the panel can say the system keeps these behind that permission.
- **MTU.** The largest packet each interface sends whole: `ifconfig` prints it, and Windows has it in GetAdaptersAddresses. It matters to uTP and to fragmentation. The panel, easy.
- **DNS servers and search domains.** `scutil --dns` on a Mac, GetAdaptersAddresses on Windows, and `resolvectl` on Linux, where `/etc/resolv.conf` often names only systemd-resolved's own stub at 127.0.0.53. Each server is the router's address, the provider's, or a public resolver's, and a handful of well-known public resolvers' addresses is enough to say which. Encrypted DNS, set in Windows' settings or in a Mac profile, is harder. The panel, easy for the servers.
- **The DHCP lease.** The server, the lease's length, when it started and when it expires, and the router and DNS servers it handed out: on a Mac `ipconfig getpacket <interface>` prints the last reply the system kept; Windows has the lease times and the server in GetAdaptersInfo; on Linux it's the DHCP client's, through `nmcli` where NetworkManager runs. The panel, easy on a Mac and Windows.
- **Proxy and VPN.** The system's proxy settings: `scutil --proxy` on a Mac, and on Windows the Internet Settings key in the registry. A VPN shows as a tunnel interface carrying the default route, which Good3's route match already finds. iCloud Private Relay can't be read, only inferred, as Good2 does when HTTPS and UDP leave from different networks. The panel, medium.
- **Metered and Low Data Mode.** Windows marks a network metered, and macOS has Low Data Mode for each network; both need native calls, the connection's cost on Windows and the Network framework's path on a Mac. A BitTorrent client should honor both. The panel, medium, productization.

## The router

- **Its address on the local network.** Good3's gateway rows have it, in each family.
- **Its MAC address.** The system's neighbor table, built as the computer talks to the router: `arp -n` with the gateway on a Mac, with `ndp -an` for IPv6, `ip neigh` on Linux, and GetIpNetTable2 on Windows, whose `arp -a` prints its headings in the system's language. A router's ports and radios usually take consecutive hardware addresses, so the one the computer sees can differ from the sticker's in its last digits. The panel, easy, now.
- **Its external address, its epoch, and the protocols it speaks.** Good5: NAT-PMP's external address and epoch, PCP's ANNOUNCE, and UPnP.
- **Its make, model, firmware, and serial number.** No one source, so the panel takes what it finds.
	- DHCP's vendor information, option 43, in the last reply the system kept from the router, so the operating system is asked and nothing is sent. The standard leaves its contents to each vendor: on a dual-stack home connection the router wrote its product name there in plain text, and another router might write binary settings or nothing. On a Mac `ipconfig getpacket` prints it; Windows may keep the options it received in the registry beside the interface's TCP/IP settings, which is to check; on Linux it's in the DHCP client's lease file.
	- UPnP's device description, when UPnP is on: the friendly name, manufacturer, model name and number, serial number, and the address of the router's own settings page. igd-next reads the description for its control addresses and keeps none of these fields, so showing them takes another crate, like rupnp, which reads any device's description, or a fetch of the description over plain HTTP on the local network, which `net_get` refuses on purpose.
	- The SERVER line in the router's answer to a UPnP search, which names its operating system and UPnP software with their versions.
	- The maker behind its MAC address, above.
- **Its internet link, over UPnP.** GetCommonLinkProperties answers the kind of link, like Cable, DSL, or Ethernet, and the upstream and downstream maximum rates the router reports; GetStatusInfo answers whether the connection is up and for how long; and the byte totals it has sent and received. igd-next carries none of these actions, and rupnp calls any action by name. The panel, productization.
- **The IPv6 prefix the provider delegated to it,** a /56 or a /60. The computer sees only its own /64, so this comes only from the router's own settings page. Never.
- **The other devices on the network.** The neighbor table lists them, but they aren't this computer's connection. Probably never.

## The provider

What's-my-IP sites name a provider from a database of address blocks, and nothing else gives the same answer. There are three ways to come close, and a fourth fact that sits beside them.

- **A database of address blocks.** MaxMind, IP2Location, and ipinfo map every block of addresses to the network announcing it and to a brand name someone curated. The brand name exists only in databases like these; no protocol carries it. Not in the client. The page could use one through a service of our own, and free datasets map blocks to networks and their registered names with no geography in them. Pinned.
- **Reverse DNS.** The name the provider gives an address, its PTR record, often carries the address and the provider's domain, like `pool-203-0-113-42.city.example.net`. The panel asks the system's resolver through a small Rust command, since `net_resolve` only goes from names to addresses. A browser can't look up a PTR record itself, so the page needs the reflector to answer the name beside the address, which keeps it on our servers, or a public resolver over HTTPS, which sends the address to someone else. A domain isn't a brand, and shared carrier addresses and IPv6 addresses often have no name at all. Both places, easy in the panel.
- **RDAP,** the registries' successor to whois. The regional registry holding the block answers over HTTPS in JSON with who holds it, by registered name, which can be an older name than the brand on the bill. RFC 7480 asks RDAP servers to answer browsers too, so both places can ask. It sends the address to a registry, which makes the registries new entries in the names and numbers manifest. `whois` is the command a person would type, so the superset test says to match it. Both places, productization.
- **The network that announces the block,** its autonomous system and number, as whois and routing sites show it. Team Cymru answers it over DNS TXT records, which `net_resolve` can't ask for, and RDAP answers it for the network. Both places, productization.

## The browser

- **Browser and operating system.** The page's box already carries the user agent. Every Mac browser reports macOS 10.15 whatever the version, and every Windows 11 browser reports Windows NT 10.0, both frozen on purpose; Chromium's client hints answer the real platform version and Safari and Firefox don't. Saying why the number is frozen is what other what's-my-browser pages leave out. The page, easy, productization.
- **Network Information API.** Chromium's `navigator.connection` gives a rough connection type and downlink speed, rounded on purpose. The page, maybe.
- **What the page can't see.** MAC addresses, interfaces, the router, the lease, time since boot: the page says the desktop client reads them, so even its limits point somewhere. The page, productization.

## Never

- **Geography.** City, region, or coordinates for an address. They have nothing to do with connectivity, and they come from a database's guess.
- **The router's settings page,** read by scraping it. Each router's page is its own, and it changes with the firmware.
